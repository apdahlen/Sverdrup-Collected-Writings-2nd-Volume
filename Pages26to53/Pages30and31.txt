STEP 1: HAZARD IDENTIFICATION

Lexical Hazards

* Menigheden: not merely “congregation” in a sociological sense, but the spiritual assembly of the regenerate; must retain ecclesiological weight.
* Aand / Aanden: Spirit; avoid psychological reduction.
* Liv: life as spiritual, divine vitality, not mere existence.
* Orden / Forfatning / Anordninger: order and constitution as external forms, not inner essence.
* Sværmerne: historically charged term (enthusiasts, radicals); must retain polemical edge.

Syntactic Hazards

* Long interrogative sequences forming theological pressure.
* Sermonic parallelism (with Tro i Hjerte, Lys i Øie, etc.).
* Clause accumulation expressing inevitability rather than ornament.

Theological Hazards

* Doctrine of the church as living body, not institution.
* Sanctification (“renser sig selv”) echoing 1 John 3.
* Order vs. uniformity as a theological distinction.

Voice Hazards

* Earnest exhortational tone addressing conscience.
* Polemical sharpness against formalism.
* Refusal of sentimental or purely historical treatment of Scripture.

STEP 2: IDIOM ANALYSIS

* “lade Ordets Lys falde ind over”: allow Scripture to judge and illuminate the present.
* “Verdens Drømme”: illusions or fantasies of the world, not neutral imagination.
* “stævner paa sin Vandring”: pilgrimage imagery.
* “Formliv”: life reduced to external structure.
* “Gud er en Ordenens Gud”: 1 Corinthians 14:33.
* “renser sig selv, ligesom han er ren”: 1 John 3:3.

STEP 3: ARGUMENT STRUCTURE IDENTIFICATION

* Opening dilemma: Is Scripture authoritative for the present church?
* Affirmation: Scripture as divine light defining true congregation.
* Description of the living church.
* Logical analogy: if individual Christianity is possible, so is communal Christianity.
* Exhortation to faith and struggle for life.
* Transition to historical-theological reflection on Luther.
* Critique of forcing new spiritual life into old forms.
* Clarification of order vs. uniformity.
* Appeal to Luther’s own liturgical flexibility.

STEP 4: BIBLICAL ECHO IDENTIFICATION

* Light imagery: John 1; Psalm 119.
* Body/head imagery: Colossians 1.
* Pilgrimage to the Father’s house: John 14.
* Sanctification language: 1 John 3.
* Order of God: 1 Corinthians 14.

STEP 5: VOICE INTENSITY PRESERVATION

* Maintain interrogative pressure.
* Retain polemical contrast between life and form.
* Preserve exhortational urgency.

STEP 6: MODAL FORCE PRESERVATION

* “skal” rendered as “shall,” not “may.”
* Rhetorical impossibility questions preserved.

STEP 7: CONSISTENT THEOLOGICAL TERM RENDERING

* Menighed = congregation
* Liv = life
* Aand = Spirit
* Orden = order

STEP 8: METAPHOR INTEGRITY PRESERVATION

* Light vs. dreams
* Living body vs. dead material
* Pilgrimage imagery retained

STEP 9: TONE CONTRAST PRESERVATION

* Sharp questioning → confident confession → exhortation → historical reflection.

STEP 10: ORAL CADENCE AWARENESS

* Long sentences retained where argumentative force accumulates.

STEP 11: ACADEMIC TRANSLATION (VOICE-PRESERVING)

Is it wrong to let the light of the Word fall fully and completely upon our own time and our own souls, so that we truly receive biblical thoughts about the congregation? Or is the Bible only a distant book of dreams, with lovely illusions that vanish before harsh reality and the sober outlook of the world?

No indeed. The Bible is the light from God, which is meant to scatter the world’s dreams—not the reverse. And in this light the congregation truly stands forth as a newly created people born of the Spirit, with faith in the heart, with light in the eyes, with love in its work, with power to cleanse itself, with the salvation of humanity and the glory of heaven as its goal.

The living congregation lives by the Word and through the Spirit. Jesus is its head, and it follows his footsteps, and it cleanses itself, just as he is pure. It is the redeemed and restored human race, which sets its course on its pilgrimage toward the Father’s house above and seeks to gather to the Father all who can be gathered.

But if it is possible that a human being can become and remain a Christian, why should it be impossible that there can become and remain a Christian congregation?

Let us believe in God and his Word and fight for life in every soul and in every congregation.

\subsubsection{Luther and Church Order}

With the Reformation, certain new conditions of life were given. The task was that this life should be allowed to grow according to its own growth and find its own distinctive form of life.

That the new life could not, without suffering damage, be forced into the old forms is something that all now know and see.

But the ossification of life often proceeds so imperceptibly that it takes a keen eye to see the danger that threatens. Moreover, it is so with us human beings that we are often more afraid of a purely external change in form and appearance than of suffering damage to the very essence and content. This is old enough and historical enough that it ought to have been known by those who govern the church. But the life of the church—or rather of the congregation, for there is no other church than the congregation—is not a life of mere form. The congregation is not dead material like wood or marble or iron, which is hewn or cast.

The congregation is the assembly of the converted and believing. In it, life is what determines everything; form is what is incidental, in many respects what is purely nonessential. This is biblical, if anything is. Yes, one says, but there must indeed be an order, for God is a God of order. Very true. But what kind of order?

Life in God is one and the same life, that is true. But is it also true that this one and the same life can express itself in only one and the same way? God is a God of order, that is true. But is he a God of uniformity? Has the God who is Lord and Creator of nature given creation countless forms of life, but prescribed for the spiritual life in humanity—of which he is also Lord and Creator—only a single form of life?

Has God made known his omnipotence by creating an exceedingly rich natural life, but made known only brute power by creating an exceedingly poor spiritual life? That was at any rate not Luther’s meaning. And if Luther has made clear what he understood by a true congregation, then he has also let us know what he believed about purely external arrangements.

In his writing of 1526 on “The German Mass,” he says specifically about the Sunday service: “Here [that is, in Wittenberg] we retain the vestments, the altar, the candles, as long as they last or as long as we find it good to change them. But if anyone here wishes to proceed differently, we let them do so. But in the true mass among nothing but true Christians, the altar ought not to remain as it is, and the priests ought always to turn toward the people, just as Christ without doubt did so at the Supper. Now, that may wait until its time.

Then preaching is held on the Gospel of the Sunday or the feast. And it seems to me that where one has the German Postil for the whole year, it would be best that one determined that the whole sermon of the day, or a portion of it, be read aloud to the people from the book—not only for the sake of those preachers who cannot do better, but also for protection against the enthusiasts and the sects; the same thing we have seen and observed with the homilies during early preaching, that it has also taken place in approximately the same manner.”
