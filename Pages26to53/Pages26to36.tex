\subsection{The Quickening of the Congregation}

Source: Editorial articles in “Folkelbladet” of November 23 and 30 and December 7, 1898. — Ed.

This section appears on pages 26–36 of the original volume. — Present Ed.

\bigskip

\subsubsection{Living Congregations}

It will be altogether justified if someone asks us what we mean when we speak of living congregations, and by what right we make use of an expression such as this.

People are already censuring and mocking the word without considering its seriousness and meaning. It would be better if they soberly examined whether there is anything evil and dangerous, unbiblical and unchristian, in this word.

For many reasons we also wish to make the preliminary observation that, for our part, we have not praised some congregations and censured others. We have said that judgment belongs to God and God’s Word. What the Word judges to be unsound and spurious is so, whether it is found among us or among others. And it is possible to discuss the question of the congregation without becoming coarse and crude.

We do not deny that we find the expression “living congregations” well suited to express what we are working for. We sincerely believe that it is a truer expression of the character and nature of the visible congregation than other words that have been used in the Church. We also believe that it is a true expression of the aim of the Holy Spirit’s work through the Word and the Sacraments; it is his work to make alive. We also believe that life is the first, most important, and most essential attribute of the Body of Jesus Christ, which is the congregation.

By the word “living” we do not wish to exclude other words that are scripturally used of the congregation, such as holy, pure, spiritual, and the like; we mean to say that all these attributes belong to the congregation only when it is living, and because God’s Spirit makes it alive. It is the life from God, the life of the Spirit, that makes the congregation a congregation, and that makes it holy, pure, and spiritual.

That the word is justified when used of the congregation according to Scripture is evident from all those places where the congregation is called Christ’s Body. For surely no one will maintain that Christ’s Body is a dead body? Between a body and a corpse there is nothing but life to make the difference. We cannot explain the word body without having to use the word life; for what is a body but so much matter as is permeated by the same life?

Still clearer becomes the testimony that the congregation is living when in many places it is spoken of as a body that grows by virtue of its connection with Christ and through the cooperation of all its individual members (Eph. 4:1–16). For a dead body cannot possibly grow.

Likewise, such words as these, that the congregation is a spiritual house built of living stones, require life in the Spirit if they are to have meaning. The same is true of such words as these, that Christ is the Vine and the believers are the branches, or that the congregation is a plant or a planting, grown from the seed of the Word, watered by the streams of grace.

In a very particular way we also claim in support of our position the word to the angel of the congregation in Sardis: “I know your works, that you have a name that you live, and yet you are dead.” For this shows that the assembly which calls itself by the name of congregation thereby lays claim to being alive. We also see that the Lord does not concede the legitimacy of this claim in the case of the congregation at Sardis, and that consequently the congregation has, properly speaking, ceased to be a congregation. This does not prevent the believers from being saved; but although there are two or three believers in Sardis, the congregation is nevertheless dead, and if no change takes place in it, then it is finished.

Yes, one says, all this is true and right; but it concerns the “invisible Church”; Christ’s Body is an exceedingly mystical expression which cannot be intended of any outward congregational organization; it concerns the “invisible” congregation.

But first of all, the visible and invisible congregation are in fact the same congregation, so that if the “Body” is the invisible congregation, then it is at the same time also the visible congregation.

Secondly, Scripture at any rate knows no more than one congregation, whether one wishes to call it visible or invisible or both.

And thirdly and finally, it is easy to see that when Paul writes about Christ’s Body and its members, he really means the assembly of believers, or the congregational organization, in each particular place. It is simply to the “congregation of God” in Corinth, with its members, that he writes: “But you are Christ’s Body and members, each for his part.” He cannot mean the believers scattered throughout the whole world. He means precisely the congregations and the believing congregational members in Corinth, and no others.

This, then, is our biblical right to speak of “living congregations.” And we consider ourselves bound to work for them, because it is altogether clear that only such living congregations are justified and truly sanctioned by God’s Word. Where the congregation is not living, there the New Testament continually addresses to it and its members the admonition: “Awake, you who sleep, and arise from the dead!” “Be zealous and repent!”

Thus, because the New Testament wills life and continually works against death, we too believe that it is a holy calling and duty to work for “living congregations.” People say that things will go badly for us, that\footnote{Our source has ``if'' instead of ``that''; but this must surely be a typographical error. --- Ed.} we shall accomplish nothing, etc., etc. Very well; if we may work for the same thing for which God’s Spirit moved the Apostles to work, then we are content with our lot.

We also conclude from this that if there have been living congregations, then the matter is neither impossible nor impractical.

What, then, is a living congregation? Nothing other than what the apostolic congregations were: a gathering of people who, through birth by the Spirit, had become partakers of the Kingdom of God, and who, by virtue of this new life, saw existence in an altogether new light, worked for altogether new ends, and understood their responsibility in a manner altogether new and different from that of other people.

So long have the papal Church and the state Church kept the congregation out of sight that it is exceedingly difficult to awaken even a faint conception of what the congregation is.

Those people were converted, and they lived and acted in a manner opposed to the world, and the world found this “converted,” turned-about manner of life to be a thoroughly backwards manner of life. If the world and its desire and pleasure had formerly been the aim of their lives, they had now turned their backs upon the world, and God and the Kingdom of God and its blessedness and glory had become the aim of life for them. If they had formerly striven to rule, it was now their endeavor to serve. If they had formerly loved themselves and their own, they had now learned to love God and to seek their neighbor’s welfare and good. If they had formerly robbed and plundered in order to acquire much, they now worked in order to be able to give and impart. If the matter had formerly been to conquer the world in order to win its honor and pleasure, the task was now to spread the Kingdom of God, so that all might become partakers of the peace and joy of faith.

In short, in every respect and in every way, the life and calling of the congregation were converted, turned about, and opposed to the life of the world; therefore they were also backwards in the eyes of the world. Nor were they any more invisible than that they awakened both hatred and persecution. For just as the Christians knew one another, so the world also knew them very well.

Yes, one says, that was then; but now the world has been Christianized, and we live in the midst of a Christian country; there can no longer be any question of such difference and opposition.

Alas, it is surely not the world that has become Christian; it is the Church that has become worldly. And one proof close at hand is this: that in many places where people speak of “biblically legitimate” congregations, the condition is such that if a person wishes to be a Christian and live as a Christian, that same person is persecuted and mocked by worldly congregational members, and not seldom also by worldly pastors.

Is it then really wrong, when the days are evil and night is drawing on, to lift eye and mind above the confusion and despair and direct our gaze toward the true and real picture of the congregation which the New Testament gives us?

Is it wrong to let the light of the Word fall fully and wholly upon our own time and our own souls, so that we truly obtain biblical thoughts concerning the congregation? Or is the Bible only a beautiful book of fantasy with lovely dreams that vanish before hard reality and clear worldly thinking?

Oh no; the Bible is the light from God that is to disperse the dreams of the world, not the reverse. And in this light the congregation truly stands forth as a newly created people of the Spirit, with faith in the heart, with light in the eye, with love in action, with power to purify itself, with the salvation of human beings and the glory of heaven as its goal.

The living congregation lives from the Word and through the Spirit. Jesus is its Head, and it follows in his footsteps, and it purifies itself even as he is pure. It is the redeemed and restored human race, which sets its course upon its pilgrimage toward the Father’s house above and desires to gather with itself to the Father all who can be gathered.

But if it is possible for a person to become and to be a Christian, why is it impossible for there to become and to be a Christian congregation?

Let us believe God and his Word and contend for life in every soul and in every congregation.

\subsubsection{Luther and Polity}

With the Reformation certain new conditions of life had been given. The question was that this life should be allowed to grow according to its own growth and find its own distinctive form of life.

That the new life could not, without suffering harm, be fastened into the forms of the old is something everyone now knows and sees.

But the distortion of life often proceeds so imperceptibly that an open eye is required to see the danger that threatens. Moreover, it is so with us human beings that we are often more afraid of a purely outward alteration in form and appearance than of suffering injury to the very essence and content. This is old and historical enough that it ought to be known by those who govern in the Church. But the life of the Church—or rather of the congregation, for there is no other Church than the congregation—is not a life of forms. The congregation is not dead material such as wood or marble or iron, which is hewn or cast into shape.

The congregation is the assembly of the converted and believing. In this assembly it is life that determines; form is accidental, in many respects purely nonessential. That is biblical if anything is. Yes, one says; but there must surely be an order, for God is a God of order. Very true. But what order?

The life in God is one and the same life; that is true. But is it also true that this one and the same life can manifest itself in only one and the same way? God is a God of order; that is true. But is he a God of uniformity? Has the God who is the Lord and Creator of nature given creation countless forms of life, but prescribed for the spiritual life in the human being—of which he is likewise Lord and Creator—only one single form of life?

Has God made known his omnipotence by creating an exceedingly rich natural life, but made known his impotence by creating an exceedingly poor spiritual life? That at any rate was not Luther’s opinion. And if Luther clearly made known what he understood by a true congregation, he also let us know what he believed concerning purely outward arrangements.

In his writing of 1526 on “The German Mass,” he says specifically concerning the Sunday service:

“Here [that is, in Wittenberg] we retain the vestments, the altar, and the candles as long as they last, or until we see fit to change them. But if anyone elsewhere wishes to proceed differently, we let them do so. But in the true Mass among none but true Christians, the altar ought not to remain as it is, and the priests ought always to turn toward the people, as Christ without doubt did at the Supper. Well, that may come in its own time.

——————

“Then the sermon is held on the Gospel for the Sunday or feast day. And it seems to me that where one has the German Postil for the whole year, it would be best to appoint that the whole sermon for the day, or a portion of it, should be read to the people from the book—not only for the sake of those pastors who cannot do it better, but also as protection against the Enthusiasts and sects; we have seen and observed from the homilies at the early sermon that this too was formerly done in much the same way.

“Otherwise, where spiritual understanding and the Spirit himself do not speak through the pastors (by this I do not wish to set limits to the Spirit; he certainly knows how to speak better than all postils and homilies), it nevertheless finally comes to this, that everyone preaches what he pleases, and that instead of the Gospel and its exposition there is preaching about blue ducks. For one of the reasons we retain the Epistles and Gospels as they stand in the postils is that there are so few spiritually gifted pastors who can treat an entire Evangelist or another book with power and profit.

——————

“We sum it all up: This and every other order is to be used in such a way that wherever abuse arises from it, one abolishes it at once and introduces another, just as King Hezekiah broke in pieces and abolished the bronze serpent, which God himself had nevertheless commanded to be made—because the children of Israel abused it. For orders are to serve the advancement of faith and love and not to harm faith.

“When they no longer do this, they are already dead and gone and are valid no longer, just as when a good coin has been debased, it is withdrawn and altered on account of the abuser; or as when new shoes grow old and pinch, they are no longer used, but are thrown away and others are bought. An order is an outward thing; however good it may be, it can lead to abuse. But then it is no longer an order, but a disorder. Therefore no order stands or has validity of itself, as the papal orders have hitherto been regarded as doing; rather, the whole life, dignity, power, and virtue of every order lies in its right use; otherwise it has no validity or usefulness whatever. May God’s Spirit and grace be with us all. Amen!”

Luther naturally saw that if it was necessary to return to the apostolic doctrine of salvation, it was no less necessary to return to the apostolic conception of the congregation and the polity corresponding to it. The one must correspond to the other.

The misfortune is surely that the question of polity within the Lutheran Church has not kept pace with the doctrinal question as well as it did when Luther fought through and dealt with the doctrine and polity of the Catholic Church in the sixteenth century.

But is it then wrong to attempt to do something now which ought to have been done before? Or should one really entertain so great a reverence for what is purely outward in the Church that one does not dare venture to place a question mark beside one thing or another concerning the outward order?

Is it to be established as an unalterable principle that if one has nothing to criticize in the doctrine, one must leave what exists exactly as it is?

Would that be Lutheran?

\subsubsection{A Little Concerning the Work for Living Congregations}

People ask, how are we to proceed in working for congregation in the biblical sense?

And of course this is an important question in this connection, one that requires its answer. We might indeed be inclined to add that one must first consider who is to work; but we must nevertheless take one thing at a time, and we may just as well begin with this matter of the work.

In the first place, then, we should like to be allowed to say that this work is of the kind that must be done continually and without interruption. Just as a person works for bodily life from cradle to grave, and the whole body works for the maintenance and preservation of life, so the work for the life of the congregation and for a living congregation is an uninterrupted work from the time the Spirit was poured out until the coming of Christ. This work cannot be disposed of with one sudden effort; it must be carried on continually. Life in the congregation must constantly be nourished, and it has so many enemies that there must also constantly be struggle in defense of life. At every time this work is also so important for the congregation as a whole and for individual souls that it is necessary always to remember the apostolic word that now is the acceptable time and today is the day of salvation, and that we must redeem the opportune time, because the days are evil.

In the second place, it is right to say that there is also a difference between times, and that our people have a peculiar position and a peculiar opportunity precisely in our time and in our country to work for the life and freedom of the congregation. Precisely now, when we have moved out of the state Church, the matter is to make use of the opportune moment to find the proper form and manner of work of the Free Church.

With these two presuppositions in view, all well-disposed readers will understand that we are neither working for something new, but on the contrary for the old apostolic life of the congregation, nor do we mean a work that can or should be done in a couple of days and then be finished, but precisely a work that continues until the whole time of grace has reached its end.

The proper work for a living congregation among us has its point of departure in infant baptism. There can be no proper order in a Lutheran congregation unless we take firm hold there. With Spirit and Baptism the congregation began; and there it also begins among us in our day with respect to each individual human being.\footnote{The author has also elsewhere expressed himself concerning the significance of infant baptism, as well as confirmation, for the congregation. Some of these statements are included in the fifth section of the present volume. — Ed.} 

But if through Baptism our children receive life in God, the life of the Spirit, so that they become living members of Jesus’ living Body, then it is clear that Baptism carries with it the high and holy obligation that this life shall be nourished and preserved by the Word of life in and through the congregation.

And here begins a work which plainly enough requires the participation of both men and women. If the little ones are not to starve to death spiritually, nor to be torn away from Christ’s Body by force and violence, or by Satan’s cunning and deceit through the seduction of the world, then all congregational members must both know their responsibility and remain watchful and put their hand to the work at the right time and in the right manner.

This is the place where, above all things, the matter is to redeem the opportune time and to begin early and continue late, yes, “without ceasing.” This is the work from generation to generation through the passing ages.

Before parents and relatives, before pastors and teachers, before the whole Christian congregation and every individual congregation, stands the Lord Jesus and commands: “Let the little children come to me and do not hinder them, for the Kingdom of God belongs to such as these.” Do they all consider what stands written, that Jesus was indignant and spoke in this way?

Is everything really being done for the children that ought to be done? Is there an example of living Christianity in every home where the children are baptized? Is there living instruction and Christianity in every congregational school? Are confirmation instruction and confirmation everywhere what they ought to be? And are the fruits such as a work of faith and love, hope and prayer has the promise of obtaining?

Let those pastors who maintain that everything is in good order with our congregations consider the matter a little. Do the children see only Christian examples in the congregations? And is there both sound nourishment and good fencing around the “lambs” in the flock? And is not confirmation, as it is practiced, in very many cases an outrageous violation of truth and holiness?

We need say no more for everyone to see that here there is room for work: more work, better work, more earnest work, and sounder work than is ordinarily carried on in the congregations. There are few Christian homes, and there is lethargy and slackness in the ecclesiastical work at this point.

We had almost said: Take care of the children, and the congregation will take care of itself. But perhaps that would not be right. Yet this much is true, that in the New Testament we find that it was often the children’s need and their need of help that drew the parents to Jesus. And if we could all awaken and see our responsibility for the children, then we would no longer be bitter enemies of “living congregations.” No, with tears we would sigh to the Lord for the outpouring of the Spirit, that we might not hinder the little children from coming to Jesus by our spiritless example and our spiritless speech.

Therefore a vigorous work among the older people is also needed for a living congregation. For if the older are to begin with the children, they must themselves have begun to walk on the way of life. For a blind person cannot lead a blind person; and whoever does not understand what the congregation is cannot lead the children on the right way in this matter.

But this work which the older people must do, and which must be done among the older people, is above all the work of living knowledge of the truth, which takes place through God’s Word. If the congregation is to be set upon its feet, the light of the Word must be allowed to shine freely and unhindered upon this matter also. And then the issue is not to seek reassurance either in Judas or in Ananias and Sapphira; rather, the issue is to awaken to earnest concern for the salvation of souls and the life of the congregation. And then the issue is to be doers of the Word and not hearers only, whereby we merely deceive ourselves. When, then, we see how the congregation was and is to be, let us repent and “do the first works,” which means precisely beginning again from the beginning, since our development has been so crooked and has led us so far away from the “living congregation.”

It is impossible to lay enough weight upon this side of the matter: a pure understanding of the congregation and an earnest pondering of God’s Word in this matter. If pastors and congregational members do not become heartily agreed on this point—that the light of the Word shall be allowed to shine, and that we will follow the light of the Word in congregational matters as well as in all other matters—then Joseph’s hurt cannot be healed, nor can David’s fallen tent be raised from the rubble.

It is true that we cannot expect abuses of centuries to be corrected in a day; but if healing takes time, then it is all the more necessary that it begin at once. And if it is the light from God that is to heal the congregation’s maladies, then let the light be allowed to shine freely and clearly, the sooner the better.

But to spread false enlightenment concerning the congregation will return in crushing judgment upon those who do it.

If, then, one sermon every third Sunday, or even every Sunday, is not sufficient, it is right to press on with “discussion meetings” and Bible reading, to gather for prayer and meditation upon God’s Word, until the congregation’s way and calling stand clear as day to all congregational members in the light of the Word.

People say that our congregations will become too small to support pastors if we are to follow the Bible’s demand. But one should take a little care how one speaks; for if the world becomes ruling in the congregation, the time will soon come when one must look for believing pastors and believing congregational members outside the so-called Lutheran Church. And one must consider that if our Church is to have a future, it must possess viability; but this depends upon spiritual life itself, not upon form. And it will be particularly profitable to take note that our youth will not be preserved within the frame of empty, hollow forms; the young demand Spirit and truth if they are to be drawn into the congregation.

It is high time that this work be carried on with power, and we are certain that the seed of truth which is now being sown concerning the congregation and its nature and work shall bear blessed fruit among our dearest people for long times to come.
