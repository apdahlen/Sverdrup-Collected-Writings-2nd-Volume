\subsection{The Quickening of the Congregation}

Source: Editorial articles in “Folkelbladet” of November 23 and 30 and December 7, 1898. — Ed.

This section appears on pages 26–36 of the original volume. — Present Ed.

\bigskip

\subsubsection{Living Congregations}

It would be entirely justified for someone to ask us what we mean when we speak of living congregations, and by what right we make use of such an expression.

The word is already attacked and mocked, without any thought being given to its seriousness and meaning. It would be better if one were to examine with sober reflection whether there is anything evil or dangerous, unbiblical or unchristian, in this word.

For many reasons we also wish to preface the discussion with the remark that on our part we have neither praised certain congregations nor censured others. We have said that judgment belongs to God and to God’s Word. What the Word judges to be unsound and illegitimate is so, whether it is found among us or among others. And it is possible to discuss the question of the congregation without becoming coarse and crude.

We do not deny that we find the expression “living congregations” well suited to express what we are working for. We sincerely believe that it is a truer expression of the nature and essence of the visible congregation than other words that have been used in the Church. We also believe that it is a true expression of the goal of the Holy Spirit’s work through the Word and the Sacraments; his work is to give life. We also believe that life is the first and essential characteristic of the body of Jesus Christ, which is the congregation.

By the word “living” we do not wish to exclude other words which Scripture uses of the congregation, such as holy, pure, spiritual, and the like; what we mean to say is that all these qualities belong to the congregation only when it is living, and because God’s Spirit makes it living. It is the life from God, the life of the Spirit, which makes the congregation a congregation, and which makes it holy, pure, and spiritual.

That the term is warranted by Scripture for the congregation is evident from all the places where the congregation is called the body of Christ. For is there anyone who would assert that the body of Christ is a dead body? Between body and corpse there is nothing other than life that makes the difference. We cannot explain the word body without having to use the word life; for what is a body other than so much matter as is permeated by the same life?

The testimony that the congregation is living becomes even clearer when it is spoken of in many places as a body that grows in strength through its connection with Christ and through the cooperation of all the individual members (Eph. 4:1–16). For a dead body cannot possibly grow.

Likewise, such expressions as that the congregation is a spiritual house, built of living stones, require life in the Spirit if they are to have any meaning. Likewise such words as that Christ is the vine and the believers are the branches, or that the congregation is a plant or a planting, brought forth from the seed of the Word and watered by the streams of grace.

In particular, we also claim as our support the word to the angel of the congregation in Sardis: “I know your works, that you have the name that you live, and yet you are dead.” For this shows that an assembly which calls itself a congregation thereby claims to be living. We also see that the Lord does not concede the legitimacy of this claim in the case of the congregation in Sardis, and thus the congregation has in fact ceased to be a congregation. This does not prevent believers from being saved; but even though there are two or three believers in Sardis, the congregation is nevertheless dead, and if no change takes place there, then it is over with it.


Yes, one says, all this is true and right; but it concerns the “invisible Church.” The Body of Christ is an exceedingly mystical expression, which cannot be meant to refer to any outward congregational organization; it refers to the “invisible” congregation.

First, the visible and invisible congregation are one and the same congregation, so that if the “Body” is the invisible, then it is at the same time also the visible congregation.

Second, Scripture, at any rate, recognizes only one congregation, whether one chooses to call it visible or invisible or both together.

Finally, it is easy to see that when Paul writes about the Body of Christ and the members, he truly means the assembly of believers or the congregational organization in each individual place. It is simply to God’s congregation in Corinth with its members that he writes: “Now you are the Body of Christ and members, each one for his part.” He cannot possibly mean the believers scattered around the whole world. He means precisely the congregation in Corinth and its believing members—and no others.

This, then, is our biblical warrant to speak of “living congregations.” And we consider ourselves obligated to labor for them, because it is altogether clear that only such living congregations are justified and truly sanctioned by the Word of God. Where the congregation is not living, there the New Testament repeatedly admonishes it and its members: Awake, you who sleep, and arise from the dead! Be zealous, and repent!

Therefore, because the New Testament calls for life and constantly opposes death, we also believe that it is a holy calling and duty to labor for “living congregations.” People say it will go ill with us, that we will accomplish nothing, etc., etc. Very well—if we may labor for the same thing that the Spirit of God drove the apostles to labor for, then we are content with our lot.

Thus we conclude this: that if there have been living congregations, then the matter is not impossible or impractical.

What, then, is a living congregation? Nothing other than what the apostolic congregations were: a gathering of people who through baptism and the Spirit had become partakers of the Kingdom of God, and who by virtue of this new life saw life in an entirely new light, labored for entirely new purposes, and understood their responsibility in a wholly new manner, different from other people.

For so long have the Papal Church and the State Church kept the congregation at a distance, that it is exceedingly difficult to awaken even a faint conception of what the congregation is.

Respectable people were converted, and they lived and worked in a manner that was opposed to the world, and the world found this “converted” way of life to be a thoroughly upside-down way of life. If the world and its desire and pleasure had formerly been their goal in life, then they had now turned their backs on the world, and God and the Kingdom of God and its blessedness and glory had become their goal in life. If they had formerly striven to rule, then it was now their endeavor to serve. If they had formerly loved themselves and their own, then they had now learned to love God and seek the neighbor’s good and benefit. If they had formerly robbed and plundered in order to acquire much, then they now labored in order to be able to give and share. If it had formerly been a matter of conquering the world in order to gain its honor and pleasure, then it was now the task to spread the Kingdom of God, so that all might become partakers of the peace and joy of faith.

In short, in every respect and in all ways the life and calling of the congregation was reversed and opposed to the life of the world; therefore it was also upside-down in the eyes of the world. Therefore it was not invisible either, but aroused both hatred and persecution. For just as the Christians knew one another, so also the world knew them quite well.

Then one says: that was then; but now the world has been Christianized, and we live in a Christian land; now there can no longer be talk of such difference and opposition.

Alas, it is probably not the world that has become Christian; it is the church that has become worldly. And a clear proof of this is that in many places where one speaks of “biblically justified” congregations, the condition is such that if a person wishes to be a Christian and live as a Christian, that same person is persecuted and mocked by worldly congregational members, not infrequently also by worldly pastors.

Is it then truly wrong, when the days are evil and it draws toward night, to lift eye and mind above the confusion and hardening, and to direct the gaze toward the true and real picture of the congregation as the New Testament gives it?


Is it wrong to let the light of the Word fall fully and completely upon our own time and our own souls, so that we truly receive biblical thoughts about the congregation? Or is the Bible only a distant book of dreams, with lovely illusions that vanish before harsh reality and the sober outlook of the world?

No indeed. The Bible is the light from God, which is meant to scatter the world’s dreams—not the reverse. And in this light the congregation truly stands forth as a newly created people born of the Spirit, with faith in the heart, with light in the eyes, with love in its work, with power to cleanse itself, with the salvation of humanity and the glory of heaven as its goal.

The living congregation lives by the Word and through the Spirit. Jesus is its head, and it follows his footsteps, and it cleanses itself, just as he is pure. It is the redeemed and restored human race, which sets its course on its pilgrimage toward the Father’s house above and seeks to gather to the Father all who can be gathered.

But if it is possible that a human being can become and remain a Christian, why should it be impossible that there can become and remain a Christian congregation?

Let us believe in God and his Word and fight for life in every soul and in every congregation.

\subsubsection{Luther and Church Order}

With the Reformation, certain new conditions of life were given. The task was that this life should be allowed to grow according to its own growth and find its own distinctive form of life.

That the new life could not, without suffering damage, be forced into the old forms is something that all now know and see.

But the ossification of life often proceeds so imperceptibly that it takes a keen eye to see the danger that threatens. Moreover, it is so with us human beings that we are often more afraid of a purely external change in form and appearance than of suffering damage to the very essence and content. This is old enough and historical enough that it ought to have been known by those who govern the church. But the life of the church—or rather of the congregation, for there is no other church than the congregation—is not a life of mere form. The congregation is not dead material like wood or marble or iron, which is hewn or cast.

The congregation is the assembly of the converted and believing. In it, life is what determines everything; form is what is incidental, in many respects what is purely nonessential. This is biblical, if anything is. Yes, one says, but there must indeed be an order, for God is a God of order. Very true. But what kind of order?

Life in God is one and the same life, that is true. But is it also true that this one and the same life can express itself in only one and the same way? God is a God of order, that is true. But is he a God of uniformity? Has the God who is Lord and Creator of nature given creation countless forms of life, but prescribed for the spiritual life in humanity—of which he is also Lord and Creator—only a single form of life?

Has God made known his omnipotence by creating an exceedingly rich natural life, but made known only brute power by creating an exceedingly poor spiritual life? That was at any rate not Luther’s meaning. And if Luther has made clear what he understood by a true congregation, then he has also let us know what he believed about purely external arrangements.

In his writing of 1526 on “The German Mass,” he says specifically about the Sunday service: “Here [that is, in Wittenberg] we retain the vestments, the altar, the candles, as long as they last or as long as we find it good to change them. But if anyone here wishes to proceed differently, we let them do so. But in the true mass among nothing but true Christians, the altar ought not to remain as it is, and the priests ought always to turn toward the people, just as Christ without doubt did so at the Supper. Now, that may wait until its time.

Then preaching is held on the Gospel of the Sunday or the feast. And it seems to me that where one has the German Postil for the whole year, it would be best that one determined that the whole sermon of the day, or a portion of it, be read aloud to the people from the book—not only for the sake of those preachers who cannot do better, but also for protection against the enthusiasts and the sects; the same thing we have seen and observed with the homilies during early preaching, that it has also taken place in approximately the same manner.”

In any case, when spiritual understanding and the Spirit himself do not speak through the pastors (I do not mean by this to set limits on the Spirit; he knows well how to speak better than all postils and homilies), the result, nevertheless, is that each one preaches what he himself wishes, and then, instead of the Gospel and its exposition, one preaches about blue ducks. For one of the reasons why we retain the Epistles and the Gospels as they stand in the postils is this: that there are so few pastors of real spiritual depth who are able to treat an entire Evangelist or another biblical book with power and profit.

We may sum it up this way: this and every other form of order is to be used in such a way that wherever misuse arises from it, one abolishes it at once and introduces another, just as King Hezekiah broke in pieces and abolished the bronze serpent, which God himself had commanded to be made — because the children of Israel misused it. For orders exist to serve the advancement of faith and love and not to harm faith. When they no longer do this, then they are already dead and gone and no longer have any validity, just as when a good coin is debased, it is withdrawn and altered because of the misuse, or as when new shoes grow old and pinch, they are no longer worn, but are thrown away and others are bought. Order is an outward matter; be it ever so good, it can lead to misuse. But then it is no longer an order, but a disorder. Therefore no order has authority in itself, as papal orders have hitherto been regarded; but all the life, dignity, power, and virtue of an order lie in its right use; otherwise it is of no value or use at all. May God’s Spirit and grace be with us all. Amen.

Luther saw, of course, that if it was necessary to return to the apostolic doctrine of salvation, it was no less necessary to return to the apostolic understanding of the congregation and to the polity corresponding to it. The one must correspond to the other.

The misfortune is surely this: that the question of polity within the Lutheran Church has not kept pace with the question of doctrine as well as it did when Luther decisively rejected the doctrine and polity of the Catholic Church in the sixteenth century.

But is it then wrong to attempt now to do something that ought to have been done earlier? Or must one truly be so afraid of the purely outward aspect of the Church that one does not dare to risk placing a question mark at one point or another concerning the external order?

Is it to be established as an inviolable principle that if one has nothing to object to in the doctrine, then one must let what exists remain as it is?

Would that be Lutheran?

\subsubsection{A little about the work for living congregations}

One asks: how shall we go about working for the congregation in the biblical sense?

And naturally this is an important question in this connection, which demands an answer. We might indeed be inclined to add that one must first consider who is to do the work; but then we must take one thing at a time, and therefore just as well this matter of the work.

In the first place, then, we may be permitted to say that this work is of such a kind that it must be done continually and without interruption. Just as a human being labors for bodily life from cradle to grave, the whole body working for the sustenance and preservation of life, so the work for the life of the congregation and for a living congregation is an unceasing work from the time the Spirit was poured out until the coming of Christ. This work cannot all be done in a single effort; it must be carried on at all times. Life in the congregation must continually be nourished, and it has so many enemies that there must also be continual struggle in defense of life. The work is at all times so important for the congregation as a whole and for the individual souls that it is necessary always to keep in mind the apostolic word, that now is the acceptable time and today is the day of salvation, and that we must redeem the time, since the days are evil.

In the second place, it is also right to say that times are different, and that our people have a particular position and a particular opportunity precisely in our time and in our land to work for the life and freedom of the congregation. Precisely now, when we have moved out of the state church, it is a matter of making use of the opportune moment to find the proper form and manner of work of the free church.

With these two presuppositions in mind, all well‑disposed readers will understand that we are not working for anything new, but rather for the old, apostolic life of the congregation; nor do we mean a work that can or should be done in a couple of days and thereby be finished, but precisely a work that endures through the whole time of grace unto the end.

For us, the proper work for a living congregation begins with infant baptism. There can never be proper order in a Lutheran congregation if we do not take firm hold there. With the Spirit and baptism the congregation began; and there it also begins among us in our own day for each individual human being.

But if our children receive life in God through baptism—the life of the Spirit—so that they become living members of Jesus’ living body, then it is clear that baptism brings with it the high and holy obligation that this life must be nourished and preserved by the Word of life in and through the congregation.

And here a work begins which clearly enough demands the participation of both men and women. If the little ones are not to starve to death spiritually, nor to be torn away from Christ’s body by force and violence or by Satan’s cunning and deceit through the seduction of the world, then all members of the congregation must both know their responsibility, be on guard, and apply themselves to the work at the right time and in the right way.

This is the place where, above all things, it is a matter of redeeming the time and beginning early and holding on late, yes, without ceasing. This is the work from generation to generation through the passing ages.

Before parents and relatives, before pastors and teachers, before the entire Christian congregation and all individual congregations, the Lord Jesus stands and commands: Let the little children come to me, and do not hinder them, for the kingdom of God belongs to such as these. Do they all consider what is written, that Jesus became angry and spoke in this way?

Is everything truly being done that ought to be done for the children? Is there a living example of Christianity in all homes where the children are baptized? Is there living instruction and Christianity in all congregational schools? Are confirmation instruction and confirmation everywhere what they ought to be? And are the fruits such as a work of faith and love, of hope and prayer, is promised to bear?

Let those pastors who assert that everything is in good order with our congregations think a little. Do the children see only Christian examples in the congregations? And is there both sound nourishment and good fencing around the lambs in the flock? And is not confirmation, as it is practiced, an outrageous violation of truth and holiness in the most manifold cases?

We need not say more for all to see that here there is room for work—more work, better work, more serious work, and healthier work than is usually carried on in the congregations. There are so many homes full of temptation, and there is sluggishness and laxity in the work of the church at this point.

We almost said: Watch over the children, and the congregation will watch over itself. But perhaps that would not be entirely correct. Yet this much is true, that in the New Testament we often find that it was the children’s distress and help that drew the parents to Jesus. And if we could all awaken and see the responsibility for the children, then we would no longer be bitter enemies of “living congregations.” No, we would with tears sigh to the Lord for the outpouring of the Spirit, that we might not hinder the little children from coming to Jesus by our lifeless example and our lifeless speech.

Therefore there is also need of a powerful work among the older generation for a living congregation. For if the old are to begin with the children, then they themselves must have begun to walk on the way of life. For a blind person cannot lead a blind person; and the one who does not understand what a congregation is cannot lead children on the right path in this matter.

But this work which the older generation must do, and which must be done among the old, is chiefly the work of a living knowledge of truth, which comes through God’s Word. If the congregation is to come to its feet, then the light of the Word must be allowed to shine freely and unhindered also upon this matter. And then it is not a question of seeking false reassurance either in Judas or in Ananias and Sapphira; but then it is a matter of awakening to earnest concern for the salvation of souls and the life of the congregation. And then it is a matter of being doers of the Word and not hearers only, by which we deceive ourselves. When we then see how the congregation was and shall be, let us turn around and do the first works—which indeed means precisely to begin anew, since our development has been so crooked and has led us so far away from the living congregation.

It is impossible to place too much weight on this side of the matter: a pure proclamation concerning the congregation and a sincere grounding in God’s Word in this matter. If pastors and members of the congregation do not become heartily agreed on this point, that the light of the Word shall be allowed to shine and that the light of the Word we will follow both in congregational matters and in all other matters, then Joseph’s wound cannot be healed, nor can David’s fallen tent be raised from the rubble.

It is true that we cannot expect abuses of centuries to be corrected in a single day; but if healing takes time, then it is all the more necessary that it begin at once. If it is the light from God that shall heal the congregation’s breach, then let the light shine freely and clearly—the sooner the better.

But to spread false illumination about the congregation will return with crushing judgment upon those who do so.

If one sermon every third Sunday or even every Sunday does not suffice, then it is right to press on with conversation meetings and with Bible reading, to gather for prayer and consideration of God’s Word, until the congregation’s way and order stand clear as day for all members of the congregation in the light of the Word.

It is said that our congregations will become too small to support pastors if we are to follow the demands of the Bible. But let one take care how one speaks; for if the world becomes dominant in the congregation, then the time will soon come when one must search for believing pastors and believing members of the congregation outside the so‑called Lutheran Church. And one must consider that if our church is to have a future, then it must have viability; but this rests on the life of the Spirit itself, not on form. And it will be particularly beneficial to observe that our youth cannot be preserved within the framework of empty, hollow forms; the young desire Spirit and truth if they are to be drawn into the congregation.

It is high time that this work be carried forward with power, and we are certain that the seed of truth which is now being sown concerning the congregation and its nature and work will bear blessed fruit among our dearly redeemed people for generations to come.
