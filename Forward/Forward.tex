\section*{Preface}

As the second volume of Professor Georg Sverdrup’s Selected Writings is presented to the public under the title “On the Congregation,” it will be necessary to preface it with a few remarks by way of orientation.

Professor Sverdrup could, in an altogether distinctive sense, make the words of the Apostle Paul his own: “But I speak concerning Christ and the Congregation” (Eph. 5:32). His entire long and busy activity had this as its great aim: the glorification of Jesus Christ through the making alive and liberation of the Congregation. And it is perhaps scarcely necessary to add here that from the depths of his heart he believed and confessed: “But this is the work of the Holy Spirit.”

Professor Sverdrup’s proper field of labor, however, was the narrow sphere of the Seminary. There he laid precious seeds in the hearts of his many disciples, seeds which have borne, and surely will yet bear still more, glorious fruit. So fully was he occupied with the work at the Seminary that comparatively seldom was time left him for more extended written works concerning the important and far-reaching truths about which so many received oral instruction from his lips. He was therefore compelled, in the main, to restrict himself to taking up the pen when it was a matter of illuminating and remedying difficulties already at hand, and of averting dangers threatening Christian life. But then he could also, as no one else among us, bring the light of God’s Word to bear upon the ecclesiastical strife and striving of the day. There will undoubtedly be those who, in the external circumstances here indicated, will see a deficiency; others, however, will regard them as an advantage.

Those, therefore, who come to the reading of this volume of Professor Sverdrup’s writings with the thought that they will find there an abstract, scholarly exposition of the question of what the Christian Congregation is, and so forth, will probably, to a certain degree, find themselves disappointed. What they will find instead are accessible expositions of a series of questions of principle concerning the Congregation, and alongside these a considerable number of contributions to the ecclesiastical discussion. But there is this distinctive feature in these contributions: in them the author always seeks to turn the gaze away from what is merely accidental and momentary and toward what is abiding and principled. Here these contributions have their very greatest significance, and herein they differ from so many other contributions to religious and ecclesiastical questions, in that they cast the light of the Word and of history upon the conditions and difficulties at hand, some of them purely local.

In this way they are also in a high degree fitted to bring into prominence the exceedingly important truth that God’s Word has an answer to every question within the sphere of church and Christian life for the one who, under the guidance of the Holy Spirit, seeks its answer, and that even the struggles and difficulties of the day have significance for the church at large and therefore ought not to be regarded merely as insignificant and wholly accidental events of exclusively local interest and value. Guided by the fundamental view here indicated, the author consistently sought to lift the gaze of his hearers and readers upward, in order to help them gain a wide and clear prospect over the Lord’s will and ways.

It has not been without difficulty to attempt to gather the many essays that make up this volume into suitable groups, and I am by no means certain that I have succeeded even approximately. The selection included here spans, moreover, a period of thirty-eight years, and it is self-evident that repetitions will occur in part. It has also been important to me, as far as possible, to follow the chronological order, in order that the author’s own view through these many years might all the better receive its due. Yet in certain sections I have considered it best to bring together material from different periods of his active service.

From what has been said above it will be understood that it has scarcely been possible to avoid the naming of persons and church bodies, at times with censure or criticism. Where I considered it necessary to include the passages concerned, I have made no substantial alterations. In this respect also, in the present volume, I have followed and intend hereafter to follow the principle which I stated in the Preface to the first volume (page VIII).

It is necessary here to call attention to the fact that this volume includes from Folkebladet a considerable number of articles which do not bear Professor Sverdrup’s signature, but which from time to time appeared as editorials in that paper. As is well known, Professor Sverdrup wrote, over the course of the years, a series of editorials for Folkebladet, and it is only after I, through investigations of my own and of others, had convinced myself of his authorship that I included a selection of these articles. There is, of course, always a possibility—though, as I believe, a very remote one—that errors may have crept in here. These articles are in every individual instance marked as editorials, thereby indicating that they lack the author’s signature.

The historical notices which I have considered it absolutely necessary to provide I have inserted at the appropriate place, either as introductory remarks to the individual sections or in the form of notes beneath the text.—The headings chosen by the author himself have, with a few exceptions, been retained.

Where groups of articles deal with the same matter, I have added such a common title as the contents seemed naturally to indicate.

The portrait of the author which accompanies the present volume was taken in 1895 and is regarded as one of the best from that period.

With regard to the publication of the work, I ask leave to state that a change has been made in the order announced in the subscription invitation, in that the third volume will bear the title: Augsburg Seminary and the Lutheran Free Church.

With the prayer that God’s blessing may rest upon it, this second volume of Professor Georg Sverdrup’s Selected Writings is sent forth to our church people on both sides of the ocean.

Augsburg Seminary, January 25, 1910.

Andreas Helland.
