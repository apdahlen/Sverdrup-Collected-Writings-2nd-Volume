STEP 1: HAZARD IDENTIFICATION

Lexical Hazards:

* "Menighed" – carries theological weight beyond "congregation"; denotes the gathered body as a spiritual organism.
* "Frikirken" – not merely "free church" generically, but the Free Church movement in Norway with ecclesiological implications.
* "Hellighed" – holiness as consecration, not moralism.
* "Arbeide" – often implies spiritual labor, not merely organizational work.
* "Læren" – doctrine as contested orthodoxy, not teaching in general.

Syntactic Hazards:

* Long cumulative sentences with multiple subordinate clauses.
* Sermonic parallelism and anaphora ("det var for Menigheden …").
* Semicolon logic that carries argumentative pivots.

Theological Hazards:

* Church as Christ’s body and bride.
* Menigheten as "Guds Hus" and "Sandhedens Piller og Grundvold" (1 Tim 3:15).
* Tension between doctrine and ecclesiology.

Voice Hazards:

* Earnest, admonitory tone toward the church and people.
* Civic–spiritual linkage between free people and free congregation.

STEP 2: IDIOM ANALYSIS

* "Sandhedens Piller og Grundvold" – direct biblical echo; must retain metaphor.
* "Salt i det frie Folk" – Matthew 5 imagery.
* "Trællesind og Slaveaand" – moral–spiritual disposition, not political slavery.

STEP 3: ARGUMENT STRUCTURE IDENTIFICATION

* Thesis: The primary unresolved question of the Free Church is the nature of the congregation itself.
* Observation: Practical work is done, but reflection and shared understanding are lacking.
* Biblical grounding: Paul’s life and writings centered on the congregation.
* Societal implication: A free people cannot survive without a free, holy congregation.
* Polemic: Doctrinal controversy masks deeper ecclesiological disagreement.
* Conclusion: Renewed, clear understanding of the congregation is necessary for unity and progress.

STEP 4: BIBLICAL ECHO IDENTIFICATION

* 1 Corinthians 12 – Body of Christ.
* Ephesians 5 – Christ and the Bride.
* 1 Timothy 3:15 – Pillar and ground of the truth.
* Matthew 5:13 – Salt of the earth.

STEP 5: VOICE INTENSITY PRESERVATION

* Warnings about societal decay must remain sharp.
* No softening of critique toward press and church leadership.

STEP 6: MODAL FORCE PRESERVATION

* Necessity and inevitability retained in phrases like "kan ikke" and "vil kunne".

STEP 7: CONSISTENT THEOLOGICAL TERM RENDERING

* Menighed = congregation
* Frikirke = Free Church
* Læren = doctrine

STEP 8: METAPHOR INTEGRITY PRESERVATION

* Preserve organic and architectural metaphors.

STEP 9: TONE CONTRAST PRESERVATION

* Move between lament, exhortation, and hope without flattening.

STEP 10: ORAL CADENCE AWARENESS

* Retain long sentences where argument accumulates.

\subsection{The Holiness of the Congregation}

Source: “Folkebladet” of February 13 and 27 and April 17, 1889. — Ed.


\subsubsection{The fundamental main question in the Free Church}

Of all the questions which the Free Church has to resolve, the foremost and most important is the question concerning the congregation itself. It is not much discussed among us, although, of course, everywhere that there is a congregation, much faithful labor is being carried on toward the solution of this question. Congregations are indeed being formed and organized in manifold places, and the Lord alone knows how many prayers are sent up, how many struggles are fought, how many sacrifices are brought, how much labor is accomplished in every single place where a congregation is gathered and built up. But in our public press, even in the church papers, little is written about the congregation; and the consequence is often that work is carried on in one way in one place and in another way in another.

One may read through year after year of church journals and perhaps not find a single piece about the congregation. One may travel from annual meeting to annual meeting and perhaps never hear a single word of instruction or guidance concerning the congregation. It is most often mentioned when there is talk of collecting offerings and contributions for church purposes; and that may be well enough; but there is far more to be said about the congregation than that it should pay. It was for the congregation that Paul sacrificed a life filled with the greatest exertions and the most painful sufferings; it was in order to found congregations that he traveled and preached; it was to strengthen, establish, guide, and build up congregations that he wrote his precious letters; it was the congregations that he expected would be the bearers of the gospel, the house of God on earth, the pillar and ground of the truth. It was the congregation he looked to as the body and bride of Christ; and he says that this mystery is great when he speaks of Christ and the congregation.

Therefore it is perhaps not too much if we attempt to consider, if only a little, the questions that directly concern the congregation. And since there is no church paper among us in which there can be said to be room for a somewhat thorough treatment of this matter, it may perhaps be excused that we must ask for space in Folketbladet, which has always had a little room to spare for the cause of the congregation among us. There may not be much room to be had there either; but a piece or two may perhaps be allowed to slip in now and then.

The matter is not irrelevant to the people, and a genuine people’s paper will therefore not pass it by in silence; but it is clear enough that the people have many other interests as well, and there can hardly be given much space to every cause. Yet a free congregation in a free people is a source of life which the free people cannot lose or do without without irreparable harm. If this source dries up, the life of the people withers as well and becomes lofty and dry and faint. A servile mind and a slave spirit unfailingly follow in the track of materialism; and what else can result than materialism in one form or another, coarse or refined, where the free congregation is no longer salt in the free people?

Attempts have also been made among us to lead the whole of church work into entirely different paths by placing the question of “doctrine” in place of the question of the congregation. What this crooked direction has cost in strife and division, the people now have ample experience of. And how much harm has been wrought will perhaps only the future fully reveal. But even behind the doctrinal controversy there has lurked a differing conception of the congregation and of its composition and work. Therefore it is likely that only a clear recognition of the nature and task of the congregation will be able to bring about a resolution of the church conflict which for so long a time has ravaged our people and paralyzed its blessed labor for the building up and growth of a free church among us.

Therefore, also with regard to the cause of union, it is well worth undertaking a renewed and thorough consideration of the question of the congregation. For it is beginning to show itself that the cause of union meets with difficulties—indeed, meets resistance—precisely for the reason that there are differing conceptions of the congregation.
