\subsection{The Holiness of the Congregation}

Source: “Folkebladet” of February 13 and 27 and April 17, 1889. — Ed.

This section appears on pages 5–17 of the original volume. — Present Ed.

\subsubsection{The Fundamental Question in the Free Church}

Of all the questions facing the Free Church, the most important concerns the congregation itself. It is not much discussed among us, although, of course, everywhere that there is a congregation, much faithful labor is being carried on toward the solution of this question. Congregations are indeed being formed and organized in many places, and the Lord alone knows how many prayers are sent up, how many struggles are fought, how many sacrifices are made, how much labor is accomplished in every single place where a congregation is gathered and built up. But in our public press, even in the church papers, little is written about the congregation; and the consequence is often that work is carried on in one way in one place and in another way in another.

One may read through year after year of church journals and perhaps not find a single piece about the congregation. One may travel from annual meeting to annual meeting and perhaps never hear a single word of instruction or guidance concerning the congregation. It is most often mentioned when there is talk of collecting offerings and contributions for church purposes; and that may be well enough; but there is far more to be said about the congregation than that it should pay. It was for the congregation that Paul sacrificed a life filled with the greatest exertions and the most painful sufferings; it was in order to found congregations that he traveled and preached; it was to strengthen, establish, guide, and build up congregations that he wrote his precious letters; it was the congregations that he expected would be the bearers of the gospel, the house of God on earth, the pillar and foundation of the truth. It was the congregation he looked to as the body and bride of Christ; and he says that this mystery is great when he speaks of Christ and the congregation.

Therefore it is not too much to attempt, even briefly, to consider the questions that directly concern the congregation. And since there is no church paper among us in which there can be said to be room for a somewhat thorough treatment of this matter, it may therefore be excused that we ask for space in Folketbladet, which has always had a little room to spare for the cause of the congregation among us. There may not be much room to be had there either; but a piece or two may perhaps be allowed to slip in now and then.

The matter is not irrelevant to the people, and a genuine people’s paper will therefore not pass it by in silence; but it is clear enough that the people have many other interests as well, and there can hardly be given much space to every cause. Yet a free congregation in a free people is a source of life that cannot be lost without irreparable harm. If this source dries up, the life of the people withers as well and becomes empty, dry, and lifeless. A servile mind and a slave spirit unfailingly follow in the track of materialism; and what else can result than materialism in one form or another, coarse or refined, where the free congregation is no longer salt in the free people?

Attempts have also been made among us to lead the whole of church work into entirely different paths by placing the question of “doctrine” in place of the question of the congregation. What this crooked direction has cost in strife and division, the people now have ample experience of. And how much harm has been wrought will perhaps only the future fully reveal. But even behind the doctrinal controversy there has lurked a differing conception of the congregation and of its composition and work. Therefore only a clear recognition of the nature and task of the congregation can resolve the church conflict. That conflict has long ravaged our people and paralyzed their blessed labor for building and growing a free church among us.

Therefore, also with regard to the cause of union, it is well worth undertaking a renewed and thorough consideration of the question of the congregation. For it is beginning to show itself that the cause of union meets with difficulties—indeed, meets resistance—precisely for the reason that there are differing conceptions of the congregation.


In addition, there are signs that fanatical and unhealthy tendencies from outside will make their way in among us. The unsound views for which Pastor Trandberg and his Reformed kindred spirits act as spokesmen easily give rise to confusion, so long as no public exchange of views takes place concerning this very fundamental question. And therefore it is to be hoped that, if the congregational question first comes under discussion, many will take their understanding of it from those who have had far better opportunity to reflect upon the matter than we have. Yet someone must speak the first word; and thus this, for lack of something better, may serve as a small introduction.

These lines may also be excused by the fact that an exposition of the congregational question has in fact been requested in Folkebladet, so that it is not unprompted that we take up a matter which we clearly see to be both too great and too weighty for our abilities and our time, but which we hope others will take an interest in elucidating in a manner we ourselves do not command.

What must be correctly emphasized this time is the great significance of the matter. If the congregations are rightly planted and rightly rooted, then all other ecclesiastical work becomes relatively easy, solid, and secure. But if the congregations themselves are loose and unstable in their composition, then so much strength is wasted merely in holding the congregations together that there is scarcely time or energy left for any other genuine work. Therefore, for the Free Church, which cannot thrive without progress and growth, it is a matter of life to have congregations formed that truly correspond to their high and holy name. And if this great task could be rightly solved, it would be of great and lasting benefit for us and our descendants. It would also be of extraordinary importance for Norway if the nature and work of the congregation were rightly and vividly set forth among us; for it will not be long before the same work which we are now doing here must also be done there, under far more difficult conditions than those with which we have to contend.

Therefore the matter is serious and great; and not least does it have such wide-reaching significance because all members of the congregation, men and women, old and young, must take part in the solution of the task if it is to be well accomplished. All therefore need to have a clear view of the congregation and a sure grasp of the work that must be done if the congregation is to receive its rightful establishment among us.

\subsubsection{What is the congregation?}

It is necessary for anyone who wishes to have a secure point of departure for the treatment of the many questions that stand in connection with the congregation first to form a clear understanding of what the congregation is.

Such questions as these: Who ought to be admitted into the congregation? Who ought to be expelled from the congregation? What demands does the congregation make upon its members? What is the pastor’s right in the congregation?—and many more questions which can and must both arise and be answered among us—can only be answered in a clear and satisfactory manner when it stands firm and clear before us what the congregation itself is.

Our catechism speaks of what the Church is, but not directly of what the congregation is. All the more does the New Testament speak of the congregation or the congregations. Now these are sometimes called parish congregations or “local congregations”; and it is not altogether free from a certain aftertaste of childishness when one speaks of “the local congregations.” We therefore permit ourselves to note that here we are speaking of “the local congregation.” And just as it is an incontrovertible fact that the New Testament speaks with the deepest reverence and holiest seriousness precisely of the local congregations and calls them God’s congregations and God’s temple and Christ’s body and Christ’s bride, so it is not too much that we also, with all reverence and respect, speak of this divine institution among us and esteem it very highly for Christ’s and God’s sake.

In order to obtain a secure answer to the question of what the congregation is, we may just as well at once go straight back to the first congregation, the one that was formed on the feast of Pentecost through the outpouring of the Spirit and Peter’s preaching. Of this event we read:

“Those who gladly received Peter’s word were baptized, and there were added that same day about three thousand souls to the congregation. And they continued steadfastly in the apostles’ teaching and the fellowship, in the breaking of bread and the prayers.”


The congregation was thus already present at the feast of Pentecost. It had not adopted any constitution, had not chosen officers, had not organized itself in any outward way by regulations and resolutions; and yet it was there, for the three thousand souls “were added that same day to the congregation.”

It is therefore simple enough, on the basis of this Word of God, to obtain an answer: What is the congregation? For it can be nothing else than, first, the gathering of those upon whom the Spirit had fallen with visible and readily recognizable marks, and then all those who, through Peter’s preaching, believed, were baptized, and held fast to the apostles’ teaching, the fellowship, the breaking of the bread, and the prayers.

It was no different with all the other congregations which were gradually established through the labor and preaching of the apostles and other believing people. These too were formed through the working of the Spirit, the Word, and Baptism, and they continued through the use of the Word and the Supper.

It goes without saying that not long after, a fixed organization also came into use in all congregations, largely uniform everywhere; yet each congregation was organized for itself, without being bound together with other congregations by outward law and order, for they were bound together by the Holy Spirit.

From this it will be understood that when the Word of God speaks of congregations, it speaks of fellowships gathered in each place by the Spirit and the Word, Baptism and the Supper. These adopted an order precisely for the sake of these things—that is, ordered themselves with the purpose of having the proclamation of the Word and the use of the Sacraments in their midst, in order thereby to save themselves and to save others with them. They were fellowships of such as, themselves converted and believing, also wished to labor for the conversion and faith of other people by the means which the Lord had given.

Therefore it is the use of the Word and the Sacraments which constitutes a congregation in the biblical sense. It is the mark and distinguishing sign of the congregation and of its members that they use the Word and Baptism and the Supper for their own salvation and blessedness and that of other people.

But, one might perhaps ask: Is not faith, true and living faith, the real mark of the congregation? Can it be called a congregation when it does not consist solely of believers or living Christians? To this the Word of God gives an altogether definite and unambiguous answer.

The congregation remains a congregation even when some of its members are hypocrites, dead, sleeping, unbelieving, and ungodly. There may be great black stains upon a congregation, and yet it does not cease to be a congregation so long as it preserves the use of the Word and the Sacraments.

It may be a poor congregation, but it does not cease to be a congregation. Yes, it may, with many sleeping members, nevertheless be a true and genuine congregation of God.

If someone cannot read this in the Word of God, then at any rate he does not read it correctly. For the congregation in Corinth was a congregation of God, “rich in everything, in all teaching and all knowledge”; and yet Paul writes of it that “many among you are weak and sick, and a great many are sleeping.” And for those among us who read the Word of God with seriousness, it is almost superfluous to mention what stands in John’s Revelation concerning the congregations in Ephesus and Pergamum and Thyatira and Sardis and Laodicea—how much was lacking in these congregations in faith, in life, in love, in zeal, and yet they were congregations, precious and beloved for Christ’s sake.

Therefore there is a difference between a true congregation and a perfect congregation, just as there is a difference between a sincere Christian and a perfect Christian. If we follow the Word of God in its guidance concerning the congregation—which alone can give us reliable guidance in this matter—then we must say that it does not abolish the congregation if it has individual dead members; but it does abolish the congregation when it abolishes the use of the Word and the Sacraments.

The Word and the Sacraments must be used, and they must be used according to their divine appointment. They must be used in order to work repentance and faith both within and outside the congregation, used to preserve, strengthen, nourish, and sustain faith and life in the congregation. Whoever therefore neglects the hearing of the Word and the use of the Sacraments thereby separates himself from the congregation and gradually ceases altogether to be a member of the congregation.

It is therefore self-evident that not all members of the congregation are saved; but it is just as self-evident that it is the calling of the congregation to labor with the purpose and the prayer that, if possible, all its members might become converted, regenerated, and saved.

It is therefore not a sleep-inducing or soul-destroying doctrine that Scripture presents, when it shows us the congregation as a fellowship of those who use the Word and the Sacraments for their own salvation and that of other people, and when it shows us that the congregation can continue to be a congregation even though it has individual dead members. But it is true that all deviations from Scripture’s presentation of the congregation are apt to produce sleep and death.

It is sleep-inducing and misleading when one teaches that the congregation is an association for maintaining a pastor and a church and a burial ground. These things belong to the work of the congregation, but they are not the essence of the congregation. And a man can be a member of such a congregation without any other requirement resting upon him from the side of the congregation than that he pays his regular contributions. It is also sleep-inducing when one thinks that the congregation is a society for preserving and maintaining pure doctrine; for in that there lies no other demand upon the members of the congregation than that they procure for themselves an orthodox teacher. It is also sleep-inducing when one thinks that the congregation consists only of the converted, and that one must go outside the congregation in order to find the unconverted. For then the one who is inside the congregation becomes secure and confident, and he calmly lets all exhortation to repentance and conversion pass him by and sends it on to those who are outside.

If one wishes to work for a living and spiritual congregation, then one must take a definite stand in the Word of God, which presents to us living and spiritual congregations, in which the Word and the Sacraments, through the powerful working of God’s Spirit, are the living sources from which the members of the congregation receive faith and life and spirit, if they use them rightly.

This biblical understanding—that the congregation is a fellowship gathered by the Spirit and the Word, without any external legal compulsion or outward enticements, and that it uses the Word and the Sacraments for its own salvation and that of others—entails that congregations must be free, active, and engaged in mission.

A congregation that has not come into being in freedom has not come into being in the right way according to Scripture. A congregation that does not exercise freedom and put the gifts of grace to use does not order itself according to Scripture. A congregation that does not labor in mission does not use the Word and the Sacraments as God’s Word requires. But even though there are in this respect great weaknesses and grave failures, still not every mark of the congregation has yet disappeared.

From this consideration of the local congregation, which we believe is firmly grounded in the Lord’s Word, we proceed in the treatment of all questions concerning the congregation. If this point of departure is not biblical, and if we have erred here, then much will become erroneous in our understanding of the work and life of the congregation. We therefore gladly ask all who understand the matter to correct possible distortions at once. For nothing can be more dangerous or more harmful for all our free-church work than to spread distorted conceptions of the congregation, its essence, and its activity.

\subsubsection{On the Holiness of the Congregation}

The question is raised whether a congregation is holy when not all of its members are believing, converted, true, reborn Christians. And there is scarcely any point at which we hear so many harsh judgments about the congregations as precisely here. For it is an undeniable fact that many belong to the congregations who are not living Christians. One points to these and to their life and conduct, and from this one draws the conclusion that the congregation to which they belong is not holy. And from this one concludes again that it is not any congregation at all, but a Babel, from which reborn and living Christians must “go out.”

Such judgments arise from the thought that the holiness of the congregation rests upon the holiness of its members. According to this way of thinking, it is the holy people who make the congregation holy. If, then, a sufficient number of holy people unite, one gets a holy congregation; but in no other way does one get any holy congregation, and therefore, in truth, no congregation at all, but a Babel.

This way of thinking has a strong appearance of godliness and at the same time appeals, precisely for that reason, to the proud human heart; for it is indeed a great exaltation for the one who can thus be among those who sanctify the congregation.

Therefore this way of thinking is also well suited to cause a great deal of harm and confusion.

First and foremost, this view does great harm to a serious contemplation of holiness itself. For if the holiness of the congregation is produced by the holiness of its members, then it amounts to very little. For even if a congregation were found that consisted only of living Christians, even such a congregation would not be sanctified by the holy conduct and fellowship of its members. For it would always be exceedingly deficient. One would then have to reduce the holiness of the congregation to a minimum, which would necessarily lead to dullness and slackness in the recognition of sin and in the struggle for sanctification, and this would be followed by a corresponding self-exaltation and self-satisfaction.

Next, this notion—that the holiness of the members sanctifies the congregation—is corrupting, because it necessarily leads to judgmentalism and strife and parties and divisions. For it is certainly true that the congregation must be holy, otherwise it is not a congregation at all, and then I cannot be saved without leaving it. If now the holiness of the congregation is to rest upon the holiness of the members, then it becomes for me, who wish to be saved, a matter of salvation to ascertain whether the other members of the congregation are holy. For from them the holiness of the congregation is supposed to come, and if now not all are holy, then either these unholy ones must be expelled from the congregation, or else I myself must leave it for the sake of my soul’s salvation. Thus begins an investigation into the holiness of the other members. And it does not take long before one finds an unclean person among the people of the congregation. And after a while one finds another, and then yet another, and soon one sees nothing but hypocrites all around. If one then finds some like-minded persons, the thought soon matures that these few, whom we know as our own, they are the true congregation; and if they cannot expose the others, then they must separate themselves, and thus one soon has the whole party-spirit with judgmentalism and heart-hostility in full bloom. One case of church flight follows after another; one division succeeds another, and amid the steadily growing confusion the congregation easily becomes the very “Babel” one wished to avoid, a home of strife where one does not understand the language of the other.

Furthermore, this view does harm in that it often drives newly awakened souls to leave a congregation rashly. They are awakened, they come to themselves and look about. They find unholiness in the conduct and fellowship of the members of the congregation; they are horrified and think: here is not God’s congregation. It is best to flee from here. If they then leave the congregation because it is not holy enough, they have thereby often done themselves much harm, and they have always deprived the congregation of the spiritual strength that was meant to benefit it and which the Lord had given them, that they might be salt and light also within the congregation. They themselves may well suffer loss thereby; the congregation will always suffer loss thereby. And sorrowful and heart-rending it often is, when God’s children in a congregation and a faithful shepherd of souls have perhaps for a long time sighed and prayed that awakening might come in the congregation, then to have to watch as the newly awakened leave the congregation, and its chamber becomes larger instead of smaller.

Furthermore, this view—that the congregation is only as holy as its members make it—does great harm to the unconverted members of the congregation. They lose their respect for the congregation; they feel no responsibility in belonging to it. They are unconcerned that their sinful life is a stain upon God’s sanctuary, for they reckon that the congregation is no holier than they themselves are, and in their crude minds they do not grasp their own shame. For they soon imagine that they are good enough for the company in which they find themselves. And when no one reminds them that the congregation is holier than all its members taken together, it is no wonder if they hold their heads higher and trample the holiness of the congregation underfoot; and if there are some poor children of God in the congregation, they try to find as many blemishes in them as possible in order to reassure themselves thoroughly that they are perfectly holy enough for the company.

Finally, only one more thing shall be mentioned here, although much could be added, and that is the harm this view does by often leading pastors to regard their congregations only as a mission field. And it is indeed true and certain that the pastor who does not regard his congregation as a mission field is a very poor pastor; but on the other hand, if he sees the congregation only in this light, then he does not nurture the respect for its holiness and the reverence for God’s house which alone can give him the right point of departure for his work with the congregation. The pastor, more than anyone else, needs to be thoroughly permeated with the conviction that the congregation is holy; but if he judges the holiness of the congregation by the holiness of its members, then he is soon tempted to despise the congregation and becomes dulled in his perception of the claim which the congregation places upon him and which he places upon the congregation. If the pastor regards himself as standing outside and above the congregation because he considers it so imperfect and unholy, then his influence and spiritual authority in the congregation will soon be at an end.

But this may suffice concerning the harm and confusion that arise from a distorted consideration of the congregation’s holiness. Many could add much more to this matter from painful experiences that occur daily.

The holiness of the Church must be believed, because it cannot be seen, and yet it is a true and living reality. The Church is holy not by its own holiness, but by God’s holiness, which is in it through the means of grace. It is an assembly of people who have dared to take up for their own use and for that of their fellow human beings the only holy thing that exists on earth: the Lord’s Word and Sacraments. This community is holy not because the people who constitute it are holy, but because the Lord is there with his Spirit and grace. He dwells in the Church through the Word and the Sacraments, and his house is a sanctuary. It is he himself who sanctifies the house. He himself is the sole source of holiness, and from him, through Word and Sacrament, holiness flows to the Church and to its individual members. If the Church rightly uses the means of grace in repentance and faith, with hearts opened to the gracious working of the Spirit, then the Spirit sanctifies it and by this his activity forms the body of Christ and himself builds the house that is joined together of living stones. Thus it is the holiness of the Church—namely, the Holy Spirit in Word and Sacrament—that makes the members holy; not the other way around. “All my springs are in you.”

But if some member of the congregation does not use Word and Sacrament for salvation, does not open the door to him who knocks, then “judgment begins from the house of God”; for God’s house must be holy, and the Lord sanctifies himself and his house by judging those who will not allow themselves to be sanctified. Yet the Lord takes his time in holding judgment; he waits with patience even for the unfruitful tree in his vineyard and does not immediately cut it down; and he often waits long for the conversion of a member of the congregation and does not immediately cut him off.

This is the faith of the saints. Thus we believe that the Church is holy, even though we see much unholiness among its members; for thus the Lord himself teaches us in his Word. Or how else could he himself say that judgment begins from the house of God, if there were no uncleanness in it, or if there were nothing but converted and true Christians in the house?

This consideration of the Church’s holiness alone is suited to give the proper fear and reverence for it among all its members. The one who wishes to be a true member of the Church does not judge its holiness by the holiness of other members, but by the holiness of Christ; and when he sees how much is lacking, he seeks refuge in the source of grace, in Word and Sacrament, in order through them to grow up to him who is the head. By this he is preserved in humility.

By this consideration of the Church’s holiness, the newly awakened will not be frightened away from the Church, even if he sees sin and unbelief among its members; for if God is there, and Christ, and the Holy Spirit, then there is holiness enough, and his prayer will be that he himself may nevertheless walk worthy of the calling with which he has been called.

By this consideration the full weight of responsibility can be laid upon the unconverted member of the congregation. For he is the unfruitful tree in the Lord’s garden, and the axe is already laid at the root of the tree; if he does not repent, it is cut down and cast into the fire and burned.

By this consideration the pastor will be strengthened for vigorous work. For he will recognize that he is not without support in the very presence of the Lord in the Word and the Spirit. The holiness of the Lord’s house will fill him with zeal to seek to win all souls for their Savior and to warn all against the impending judgment. In the very holiness of the Church there is for him both responsibility and power. He is driven to labor with diligence for the edification of the Church and never to look through his fingers at its defects and shortcomings; and when he reproves and exhorts, it is not merely because he so earnestly desires to save souls, but also because the holiness of the Church demands of him that he not calmly look on while it is being defiled.

Thus a serious and Christian consideration of the congregation’s holiness is suited to form a firm point of departure for the work in the Church and to give it the proper weight and the proper authority. But must then all sinful behavior be tolerated in the Church, and must no profane person be cast out of the Church?

Yes, there must indeed be church discipline; but not in order thereby to sanctify the Church. Congregation discipline has regard to other matters; it proceeds from the congregation’s holiness, it does not bring the Church or its holiness into being. But of this, another time. First, other questions concerning the congregation must be addressed.
