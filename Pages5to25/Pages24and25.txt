STEP 1: HAZARD IDENTIFICATION

Lexical Hazards:

* "Menighed" / "Menighedsliv" / "Menighedsfrihed" — ecclesiological terms with apostolic and Lutheran resonance; must not be reduced to "congregation" in a merely sociological sense.
* "Arbeidere" — spiritual workers/laborers, not generic employees.
* "Hverv" — calling or commission with theological weight.
* "frisindede Præster" — not liberal in a modern sense, but spiritually liberated.
* "frigjorte Præster" — freed in conscience and office, not politically emancipated.
* "humanistiske Præster" — rationalistic or anthropocentric clergy, not Renaissance humanists.
* "fuglefri" — outlawed, free from legal protection; metaphorically potent.
* "Stiftelse" — foundation or institution in apostolic sense.

Syntactic Hazards:

* Extremely long periodic sentences with layered subordinate clauses.
* Semicolon and comma logic that accumulates argument.
* Rhetorical progression embedded within syntax.

Theological Hazards:

* Doctrine of ministry vs. priestly domination.
* Apostolic foundation of the church.
* Suffering as necessary to advancement of God’s cause.
* Biblical images: cross-bearing, Jordan crossing, falling in the wilderness.

Voice Hazards:

* Prophetic, polemical Lutheran sermonic tone.
* Sharp rebuke of clerical misuse of power.
* Eschatological and generational horizon.

STEP 2: IDIOM ANALYSIS

* "vandre uden Gud i Verden" — biblical idiom (Eph. 2) for life apart from God.
* "tage Korset op" — direct Christological call to discipleship.
* "falde i Ørkenen" — allusion to Israel’s unbelief (Hebrews 3–4).
* "over Jordan" — entry into promise; spiritual risk and obedience.
* "Staden paa Bjerget" — Matthew 5; public witness of the church.
* "fuglefri" — culturally bound image of being cast out yet paradoxically freer.

KJV-style equivalents help, but modern pronouns must be retained.

STEP 3: ARGUMENT STRUCTURE IDENTIFICATION

* Thesis: True congregational life and freedom arise when ministry and congregation are rightly ordered.
* Support: Evidence from functioning congregations.
* Conflict: Opposition from humanistic clergy.
* Example: Hatred toward Augsburg Seminary.
* Reversal: Persecution strengthens the cause.
* Principle: No great cause advances without suffering.
* Appeal: Self-examination of believers.
* Conclusion: Eschatological question posed to the generation.

STEP 4: BIBLICAL ECHO IDENTIFICATION

* Ephesians 2 — walking without God.
* Synoptic Gospels — taking up the cross.
* Hebrews 3–4 — falling in the wilderness.
* Matthew 5 — city on a hill.
* Old Testament — Jordan crossing imagery.

STEP 5: VOICE INTENSITY PRESERVATION

* Retain polemical edge.
* Preserve prophetic warning and consolation.
* No rhetorical softening.

STEP 6: MODAL FORCE PRESERVATION

* Maintain necessity and inevitability in claims.
* Preserve conditional–consequential logic.

STEP 7: CONSISTENT THEOLOGICAL TERM RENDERING

* Menighed = congregation (theological sense)
* Frihed = freedom (spiritual, ecclesial)
* Stiftelse = foundation

STEP 8: METAPHOR INTEGRITY PRESERVATION

* Maintain biblical geography metaphors.
* Preserve light, fruit, building imagery.

STEP 9: TONE CONTRAST PRESERVATION

* Severity → hope → warning → exhortation.

STEP 10: ORAL CADENCE AWARENESS

* Long sentences preserved where argument accumulates.
* Sermonic rhythm maintained.

STEP 11: FINAL TRANSLATION

If there come to be as many laborers in the congregation as there are believing members of the congregation, and if the pastor regains his proper Christian place in the congregation, namely, to be the one who in a particular way has the charge of binding together and strengthening the believers, of instructing the ignorant, of reminding and admonishing the careless, of calling and inviting those who walk without God in the world to come into the kingdom of God—then congregational life and congregational freedom begin to arise. There are some places also among us where quite powerful signs are evident that people have gained sight and discernment in these matters. And it appears that precisely in such congregations things go best in every respect.

And yet it is this apostolic foundation of the congregation that is so hated by all the humanistic pastors, old and new alike, because it strips the clergy of the worldly and carnal loftiness which they have seized for themselves through centuries of abuse. For this reason Augsburg Seminary has also been so hated by many, because it has labored so faithfully to educate pastors who could be in harmony with the free congregation and truly be fit for its work. And now, since by God’s undeserved grace Augsburg has succeeded in sending out a small band of liberated pastors who, throughout the congregations, labor for life and freedom—now the storming has begun in earnest, and the battle cry sounds from the great majority: Away with them, away with the Augsburgers!

One does not consider that when the free-minded pastors have been driven out of the lofty and powerful bodies, one has also torn away their bonds and removed constricting considerations, so that the expelled will soon become outlaws and thereby be able to carry out all the more powerful work for the cause of the congregation.

Never has a great cause been advanced without great suffering. Those who take offense at the afflictions can be given no better counsel than that they examine themselves, whether they have truly taken up the cross and are walking in the footsteps of Jesus. And this cause is at present the greatest cause of our people and of our church: that the congregation may gain its proper foundation among us. If this succeeds, then the sufferings that come upon us will be of no account compared with the blessing, generation after generation, that will flow from the congregation’s true liberation. If the city on the hill is built firm and high, seen far across the land through God’s Word and the love of Jesus Christ, then surely the light will carry blessing with it to souls both near and far, both at home and abroad in heathen lands. And through long ages the fruits will show themselves of the work the Lord did also in our days, when he led his congregation out into the freedom of the Spirit and of faith, into the freedom of life and of love.

Are we ready to go “over the Jordan”? Or are we merely the generation that “falls in the wilderness”? What do the believers in our congregations think?
